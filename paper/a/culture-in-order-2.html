I received a reply to my first page of this. This page will serve as a reply to the reply.

After yapping for a while to get things clear, I'll talk about the part which the author says they are the most curious about. I talked about this global system, the global dominant culture. The author raises the point that there is a huge gap of wealth and power between a middle-class Bangladeshi student (me) and the people in power in the USA. They make the decisions, I am very small in that scale. So why am I so "unquestioning" of that? The author also mentions that it is the same global system that is responsible for large scale near-extinction actions. So I would like to ask, which system, out of any system is working as a speedbump to whaling?

Question number 2: whose gravity is influencing the anti-whaling movement? The billionaires / US politicians / someone else?

Firstly, I'm not arguing out of emotional pity for whales. I'm just mapping the mechanisms and seeing where that leads us. Although my normative circle does value them not getting hunted, and that collides directly with what the Makah (or some large group like Japan) want.

This global system that I talk about, it isn't a monolith. It is broken into many many pieces. One piece is one company, another piece another company from another field of industry, another would be a random third-world village community or a unit family from a small town, then there might be a group consisting of South Asian liberal university students. Each group of individuals have patterns of thinking and doing that can be traceable throughout that group (obviously). All these groups collide with each other with their interests and values. Their interests and patterns fight for dominance. Some merge, some get destroyed.

Getting away from this abstract thinking and let's look at the sequence of events that shifted us from pro-whaling to the current anti-whaling consensus.

Back in the days whaling was simply seen as nothing to require to be put too much thought into. Whale meant energy and industrial fat. But then a few things happened - substitute oils replaced its demand and the most profitable whales became scarce. So many of the big western countries decided to exit from whaling. By the early 1970s only Japan and USSR were left among the big hunters.

Once US and UK shut down their own dead whaling industries, it became loss-less for them to put a ban on the act globally. Songs started coming out of the US that influenced people to empathize with whales more. Then it became a political / diplomatic tool to win against the other parties.

International Whaling Commission (IWC) was founded in 1946 with whaling nations to manage quotas so the world wouldn't run out of whales to kill (and probably nobody thought about banning it altogether back then). Changing any rule of IWC required 75% vote. Any sovereign country was able to become a member of the commission. So by 1982, the dominants were non-whaling countries. And they voted to put indefinite moratorium on commercial whaling.

However, that vote had no power of its own. Since, any country could file objection within 90 days and ignore the ban. So that's exactly what Japan, USSR, Norway and Peru did. But US forced them to fold up their hunting game to some degree.

And now here we are, with the anti-whaling consensus.

This far you might be little confused, what exactly am I trying to defend with this? Well, the author complained that I didn't talk about why whales are currently short in number. So I did.

Now then, I noticed that the author repeatedly mentioned capitalism in a way like, the survival of whales and Makah are both fighting against capitalism.

I think my thoughts can be better expressed with a lil bit of academic jargon. See Global Assemblages (Aihwa Ong & Stephen Collier, 2005). Yeah, it's pretty much that. From this perspective, to say "against capitalism" is rather vague way of thinking about it. I think it's wrong. Also, I gotta clarify that by Global System, I did not mean Capitalism, or any system of that sort. This global system is more like just what the world is doing. It's a mesh with no form, where lots of subsystems collide in different shapes and sizes. Which means that there is no single master system at the top. It's fluid, it's moving, always shape shifting. This "capitalism" thing is more of a subset to that system.

Another thing is, I don't believe that life is fighting against corporate capital, business, and the greed for profit. Also a very unclear way of thinking about it. Life is taking part in those and fighting against the interests of elements (another human, or company, or community) that contrast with it. Life is fundamentally something that has needs, that fights for its needs. It's greedy and whatnot. Cause funny enough, between 1948 and the late 1970s, a non-capitalist, state-socialist planned economy that is Soviet Union, it conducted one of the largest environmental slaughters in modern history. USSR didn't even have much market demand. They just slaughtered the whales just to hit their bureaucratic five-year plan of tonnage quotas.

In the history of events, some things are bound to happen. Whaling was bound to happen. Probably not a single person back in the days thought differently about whales. But then we learned, and we had to adapt ourselves to the changing situation. And this "capitalist" thing was also inevitable because of the structural scale of things being so industrially gigantic.

This too shall pass.

As the world shifts, the so-called capitalist world as we know it will eventually cease to be how we know it now. It is inevitable. (Otherwise moral pressure or activism only have much impact when the real shifting conditions create that crack in the wall.)

Coming back to the "we" question of the author. For this, first see Disjuncture and Difference in the Global Cultural Economy (Arjun Appadurai, 1990/1996). Having less wealth and power than an American billionaire doesn't stop me from independently sharing the same normative circle on a matter like this. Well, imagine a billionaire and a theoretical middle-class Bangladeshi young man both of whom have intersecting thoughts, views, priorities, hobbies, and so on. Both are humans after all, right? Not aliens too different from each other. And regardless of through which medium both of them pursue their behavioral and mental patterns, there is something underlying you can trace between people. My point is that you can create a venn diagram out of the patterns of every person, and you will find like minded groups. A Bangladeshi student and a rich American can sit inside the exact same circle of that Venn diagram. That is the answer to the author's "us / we".

Anyways back to Makah — we have to be honest about what we as independent entities / groups want. People fall in a moral dilemma in cases like this because they assume that an "ethics-first" referee-like objective position exists above everything, and that we will get the single "correct" answer if we just sympathize with everything hard enough. (Contrary to which I have said earlier that sympathy is problematic). See Raymond Geuss - Critique of "Ethics-first" Politics (Philosophy and Real Politics, 2008). There is no paradox to solve. There are only competing cultures / coaliations for the hegemonic crown.

All in all, my solution to any "moral dilemma" or "decision paradox" is committing to whichever side my normative circle stands behind. You have to stand up for who you and your group will be. Which, for the Makah-Whale case, for almost all of us (including some of the Makah folks themselves) is that we prioritize the whales, and you know why that is. You have your reason. Whichever side you pick, just don't try to become a referee. You can't. It would be more harmful that way. Another way to look at this is that by letting Makah hunt whales you are acting as a force for their culture. It's upto you if you want to be that.

As Ruth Chang says in her Hard Choices,

“

When our given reasons are on a par, as they are in hard choices, we have the normative power to create will-based reasons for ourselves to choose one alternative over the other. It is not facts beyond our agency that determine whether we should lead this kind of life rather than that, but us. In this way, we make ourselves who we are through the exercise of our normative powers in hard choices.

A friend of mine, who is now no more, once told me that, "No ones gonna answer it for you... It doesnt matter which answer you get or cling to. Just do something." Of course, the conversation with her was on a different topic, but I think that applies here as much as it applies in someone's life.
